📈 Website visitors

Who is finding getwren.co.uk and the phonics app — which pages they read, which files they download, where they came from, and how often somebody shares a link. Counted from the web server's own log, with no cookies and no visitor tracking.

What counts as a visitor here

About four in five requests for a page on getwren.co.uk are not people. Some are honest robots that say so — Google, Bing, Apple, and the AI crawlers. The rest is a scraper farm that rotates ordinary Chrome user-agents across thousands of addresses, one request each, and no rule about the browser name can tell those from a real reader.

What does tell them apart is behaviour. A real browser goes on to fetch the page's stylesheet, its script and its fonts; the scraper takes the HTML and leaves. So:

  • A page view is one request for a readable page that the sender then backed up by loading at least one of the page's own files.
  • A visitor is one address-and-browser pairing seen on one site on one day. Not a person, and not followed across days — two days of visits by the same person count as two. A week's figure is the sum of its days, so somebody returning on Tuesday counts again.
  • A download counts when its sender behaved like a browser that day, or when it arrived from an off-site link — a PDF runs no script of its own, so nothing on the page can vouch for it.
  • One address and browser reading more than fifty pages in a day is treated as a robot, however convincing it looks. On the ten days measured when this was built, the busiest were 378 and 60 page views and both came from a data centre.
  • A share is a link-preview fetch. When somebody pastes a link into WhatsApp, Facebook, Telegram or Slack, that service fetches the page to build the little preview card. It counts the paste, not how many people then clicked.

No cookies, and no IP addresses kept. To count a day's visitors once each, the job hashes the address and browser with a random salt made fresh for that day. The hashes and the salt are both deleted the moment the day closes, which leaves the count and no way back to anybody.
